Hispano vs Latinx

  1. "Hispanic" - A clonial-linguistic category
    • Origin: The term Hispanic comes from Hispania, the Roman name for the Iberian Peninsula. It was institutionalized by the United States in the 1970s Census to group together people connected by the Spanish language.
    • Focus: Language and colonial origin — it refers to people from Spanish-speaking countries or with ancestry from them.
    • Exclusions: This term excludes Brazil (which speaks Portuguese) and Indigenous or Afro-descendant communities who may not speak Spanish or reject the colonial identity tied to Spain.
    • Colonial baggage: “Hispanic” centers Spain, not the Americas. It reinforces linguistic whiteness and the erasure of Indigenous and African roots that exist across Latin America.

    In short, Hispanic tells us who was colonized by Spain — not who someone is culturally or ancestrally. It’s an imperial label, not an organic identity.

  2. “Latinx” — A Decolonial and Gender-Expansive Intervention
    • Origin: Latinx emerged from queer, trans, and feminist communities—especially youth and scholars in the early 2000s—as a gender-neutral alternative to “Latino/Latina.”.
    • Focus: Geography and anti-binary liberation — it refers to people from, or with ancestry in, Latin America (Central America, South America, and the Caribbean).
    • Inclusions: This includes Brazil, Haiti, and Indigenous and Afro-Latin communities, who were excluded under “Hispanic.”
    • Political meaning: “Latinx” is not just inclusive grammar; it’s an act of resistance. It disrupts patriarchal, binary Spanish language norms and reclaims Latin American identity from colonial linguistic control.

    Still, many within Latin America prefer Latine or Latinoamericano/a because “Latinx” can feel U.S.-centric or linguistically foreign. The struggle itself — over how to name identity — is part of ongoing decolonial transformation.

  4. Decolonial Perspective

    5. From a decolonial framework — as described in The Decolonial Awakening and The Ultimate Guide to Understanding “Whiteness” — the crucial distinction is not simply vocabulary, but power.

    • Hispanic centers the colonizer (Spain).
    • Latinx attempts to center the colonized and queer the language of empire.

    Neither term is perfect, but Latinx (and increasingly Latine) emerges from below — from those reclaiming identity, rejecting patriarchal grammar, and refusing invisibility.
