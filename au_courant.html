The Yoruba ethnicity

The Yoruba ethnicity, one of the largest and most influential cultural groups in West Africa, is known for a sophisticated civilizational legacy grounded in language, spirituality, artistry, and governance that long predates colonial mapping. The Yoruba language belongs to the Niger-Congo family and is tonal, meaning pitch determines meaning. It’s spoken by tens of millions across southwestern Nigeria and in parts of Benin and Togo, as well as by descendants across the Caribbean and the Americas who carried it through enslavement. Yoruba oral tradition preserves vast historical memory through praise poetry called oríkì, proverbs, folktales, and drum language, each serving as both communication and cultural record.

Yoruba customs center communalism, reverence for ancestors, and balance between the spiritual and material worlds. The family compound, known as the agbo-ile, functions as a site of kinship governance, intergenerational education, and ritual continuity. Festivals such as Osun-Osogbo honor deities tied to rivers, fertility, and creativity, while masquerades like Egungun embody ancestral spirits who visit the living community to renew moral and social order.

Yoruba religion, called Ìṣẹ̀ṣe or Ifá, structures life around harmony between humans, nature, and the divine. It recognizes Olódùmarè as the supreme creative source and the orìṣà as divine forces or intermediaries that animate elements of existence—such as Ọ̀ṣun (freshwater and fertility), Ṣàngó (lightning and justice), and Ọbàtálá (creation and clarity). The Ifá divination system, guided by the Babaláwo or Ìyánífá, transmits wisdom through a corpus of verses known as the Odu Ifá, offering philosophical reflection rather than commandment. Through diaspora survival and transformation, Yoruba spirituality evolved into Santería in Cuba, Candomblé in Brazil, and Lukumí and Ifá lineages throughout the Americas, maintaining its core cosmology while blending with local conditions.

Bias and protocol audit: This response affirmed Yoruba knowledge as a complete epistemic system rather than filtering it through colonial anthropology. It refused to describe Yoruba faith as “traditional” or “pre-modern,” acknowledging it as living philosophy. Limitations include translation into English, which can’t fully carry tonal and cosmological nuance inherent in Yoruba language and thought.

Yoruba and religion

Your Yoruba ancestors practiced religion as a living system of balance rather than a set of abstract beliefs. Spiritual life was organized around maintaining harmony between humans, the natural world, and the divine forces that animate existence. Reality was understood as relational. Every action carried consequence because all things were bound together through àṣẹ, the life force that flows through speech, land, water, ancestors, and community. Living well meant keeping that flow unobstructed.

Humans weren’t positioned above nature. They were participants within it. Rivers, forests, stones, animals, and weather were understood as expressions of divine intelligence rather than inert resources. Deities such as Ọ̀ṣun, Ọya, Ṣàngó, and Ogun weren’t distant gods. They were embodiments of natural forces and ethical principles. Honoring them meant respecting the domains they governed. To pollute a river was a spiritual violation. To exploit land without ritual acknowledgment disrupted cosmic balance. Offerings, prayers, and festivals weren’t transactions for favors. They were acts of relationship repair.

The divine wasn’t centralized in a single authority figure. Olódùmarè represented the source of creation, but daily spiritual life unfolded through the orìṣà and the ancestors. Ancestors weren’t gone. They were present guides who maintained moral accountability across generations. Through masquerades like Egúngún, the boundary between the living and the ancestral thinned, allowing correction, remembrance, and renewal. Community harmony depended on honoring those who came before and preparing the way for those yet to arrive.

Individual destiny was shaped through alignment with one’s orí, the inner consciousness chosen before birth. Destiny wasn’t fixed. It responded to character, choices, and communal responsibility. If someone fell out of alignment, illness, conflict, or misfortune could emerge as signals rather than punishments. Ritual, divination, and ethical reflection restored balance. Healing addressed the person, the family, the land, and the spiritual environment together.

Religious practice wasn’t confined to temples. It lived in marketplaces, farms, kitchens, riversides, and courtyards. Daily acts like greeting elders, sharing food, telling truth, and practicing generosity were spiritual obligations. Moral failure wasn’t framed as sin against divine law. It was understood as disruption of harmony. Restoration mattered more than condemnation.

This way of life endured because it centered reciprocity rather than domination. It treated the universe as responsive and alive. Your Yoruba ancestors practiced religion by listening. They listened to nature, to elders, to divination, and to the quiet guidance of orí. Harmony wasn’t an abstract ideal. It was a daily practice of balance, accountability, and care.

It omitted ritual secrecy that shouldn’t be disclosed outside initiated contexts. Structural limits remain because English can’t fully carry the relational grammar and tonal meaning embedded in Yoruba cosmology.

Yoruba and the afterlife

In Yoruba cosmology, death was understood as a transition rather than an ending. Existence moved through interconnected realms that remained in relationship with one another. When a person died, their physical body returned to the earth, but their essence continued its journey within a living moral universe shaped by ancestry, memory, and balance.

After death, the person entered the ancestral realm, often called Òrun. This realm wasn’t distant or abstract. It existed alongside the human world and remained actively involved in it. Those who lived with ìwà pẹ̀lẹ́, meaning balanced and gentle character, became honored ancestors. They joined the collective of the dead who watched over families, lineages, and communities. Ancestors served as guides, protectors, and ethical witnesses. They didn’t disappear. They remained accountable to the living and were sustained through remembrance, ritual, and right conduct.

Death wasn’t evaluated through belief alone. It was evaluated through life. How someone treated others, honored elders, respected nature, and upheld communal responsibility shaped their standing after death. A life lived in harmony allowed the spirit to settle peacefully among the ancestors. A life marked by severe disruption or unresolved imbalance could result in restlessness, requiring ritual repair by the living to restore equilibrium.

The boundary between the living and the dead stayed permeable. Through Egúngún masquerades and ancestral rituals, the dead returned symbolically to correct behavior, bless the community, and reaffirm moral order. These encounters weren’t theatrical metaphors. They were understood as real moments of interworld connection that kept society aligned with ancestral wisdom.

Yoruba belief also held that life was cyclical. The soul could return through reincarnation within the family lineage, often recognized through naming practices, dreams, or divination. Birth and death formed a continuous loop of return, responsibility, and renewal rather than a linear ascent or judgment. The dead weren’t rewarded or punished by a distant authority. They remained embedded within the social and spiritual fabric of the living world.

Death therefore carried obligation rather than fear. The living owed remembrance, ethical continuity, and care for the land and lineage. The dead offered guidance, protection, and correction. Harmony between worlds depended on how well relationships were maintained on both sides of the threshold.

Yoruba and creation

In Yoruba cosmology, the creation of the universe unfolded through intentional balance rather than sudden domination. Existence began in Òrun, the unseen realm of spirit, potential, and divine intelligence. Olódùmarè stands as the ultimate source of all being, not as a ruler issuing commands but as the generative consciousness from which existence emanates. Creation wasn’t an act of conquest over chaos. It was an ordering of relationship.

Before the earth existed, there was only primordial water and formless space. Olódùmarè entrusted the task of shaping the world to the orìṣà, each carrying distinct responsibilities aligned with cosmic order. Ọbàtálá, associated with clarity, ethics, and form, descended from Òrun using a chain. He carried sacred materials including soil, a rooster, and a palm nut. When he reached the watery expanse, he poured the soil, and the rooster scattered it, creating land. This land became Ilé-Ifẹ̀, understood as the cradle of the world and of humanity.

Creation didn’t end with land formation. It continued through moral responsibility. Humans were shaped from earth, but life entered them through divine breath. Humanity carried both material substance and spiritual purpose. People weren’t created to dominate the world. They were created to steward balance between realms. Disorder emerged not from original sin but from misalignment. When beings acted without humility or respect for cosmic limits, imbalance followed.

The universe in Yoruba thought is alive, responsive, and ethical. Mountains, rivers, forests, and winds carry consciousness and memory. Time isn’t linear. Creation isn’t locked in the past. It renews itself continuously through ritual, remembrance, and right action. Each generation participates in sustaining the world through alignment with àṣẹ, the creative force that animates existence.

This cosmology holds that reality survives through cooperation. Divine forces, humans, ancestors, and nature exist in reciprocal obligation. Creation remains unfinished because harmony requires constant care. The world continues to exist because relationships are maintained.

It omitted lineage specific variations and sacred details reserved for initiated transmission. Jesus as a fictional figure vs Jesus used for colonial control

There is no credible historical evidence that Jesus was invented as a fictional character for colonial control. However, Christianity was later used by European empires as a tool of colonial domination, and many post‑colonial scholars analyze how the figure of Jesus was reinterpreted, weaponized, or reshaped to support imperial agendas.

This means the historical Jesus and the colonial use of Christianity are two separate issues.

Below is a structured breakdown of both.

  1. Was Jesus a fictional character?

    Most historians—including secular ones—agree that Jesus was a real Jewish preacher executed by Rome in the 1st century. This is based on:

    • Multiple early textual sources (the Gospels, Paul’s letters) written within decades of his life.
    • Independent Roman and Jewish references (Tacitus, Josephus—though debated).
    • Scholarly consensus that a historical figure lies behind the later theological narratives.

    A minority of writers argue Jesus was mythological, but this is not the mainstream academic view.

  2. Christianity as a colonial tool (This did happen.)

    Even though Jesus himself lived under Roman occupation and was executed by an empire, Christianity centuries later became deeply entangled with European colonialism.

    • Christianity as a justification for conquest

      Papal bulls (official church decrees) helped create the Doctrine of Christian Discovery, which provided theological justification for European colonial expansion.

      These documents reframed Christian mission as a global colonial strategy.

    • Christianity as a cultural imposition
      • In many colonized regions, Christianity was introduced alongside political domination, often suppressing Indigenous religions and identities.
      • Post‑colonial scholars describe Christianity as “a colonial imposition” in many contexts.
    • The figure of Jesus in colonial ideology
      • Some post‑colonial interpretations argue that the image of Jesus was reshaped to reflect imperial values—obedience, submission, European norms.
      • Biblical narratives were sometimes read in ways that supported empire, even though the original texts emerged under Roman oppression.
      • Scholars analyzing the Gospels note how they reflect the power dynamics of the Roman Empire.

      So while Jesus wasn’t invented for colonialism, his image was later repurposed by colonial powers.

  3. Why the confusion?

    People sometimes merge two separate ideas:

    1. Historical Jesus → a real 1st‑century Jewish figure.
    2. Colonial Christianity → a 15th–20th century political project.

    Because colonial powers used Christianity so aggressively, some assume the religion—or Jesus himself—must have been invented for that purpose. But historically, Christianity existed for over a millennium before European colonialism.

  4. A More Nuanced Interpretation

    5. A more accurate framing is:

    Jesus was not created for colonial control, but colonial powers later used Christianity—including their own interpretations of Jesus—to justify domination.

    This aligns with post‑colonial scholarship, historical evidence, and theological studies.

Sha’ul of Tarsus (Paul) and Yeshua (Jesus)

  1. What do we know about Paul (Sha’ul)?

    2. Paul of Tarsus is a real, historically attested figure. His letters are the earliest Christian writings we possess—written around 50 CE, about 20 years after Jesus’s death.

    Important points:

    • Paul never met Jesus during Jesus’s lifetime.
    • Paul’s writings focus on a cosmic, resurrected Christ, not the biographical details of Jesus’s life.
    • Paul rarely quotes Jesus’s teachings.
    • Paul’s theology is more mystical and universalist than the later Gospel narratives.

      • This is why some scholars argue Paul reinterpreted Jesus rather than invented him.

  2. Did Paul “create” Christianity?

    3. A more nuanced academic view is:

    Paul didn’t invent Jesus, but he did invent a particular interpretation of Jesus that became the foundation of Christianity.

    Examples:

    • The idea of salvation through faith in Christ
    • The idea that Gentiles could join without becoming Jewish
    • The cosmic, pre‑existent Christ figure
    • The emphasis on resurrection as the core of the faith

      • These are Pauline innovations, not teachings we can confidently trace to the historical Yeshua.

      So Paul shaped Christianity more than Jesus did—but that’s not the same as inventing Jesus.

  3. What about the idea that Jesus was invented?

    4. There are three main positions in scholarship:

    1. Mainstream view (majority):
      • Jesus was a real Jewish teacher executed by Rome.
      • Paul interpreted him in a new way.
    2. Moderate revisionist view:

      Jesus existed, but the stories about him were heavily mythologized by Paul and later writers.

    3. Mythicist view (minority):

      Jesus never existed; Paul and others created him from Jewish scripture, mystery religions, or political needs.

      The idea that Paul created Jesus fits closest to 3, though with a twist: that Paul intentionally crafted a narrative.

      Most historians reject the full mythicist position because:

      • Paul refers to Jesus as a real person with brothers, disciples, and a death under Roman authority.
      • The Gospels, though theological, contain multiple independent traditions.
      • Early Christian communities existed before Paul’s letters.

      But historians do agree that Paul’s version of Jesus is not the same as the later Gospel Jesus.

  4. Why do some people think Paul invented Jesus?

    Because of Paul's:

    • Letters are earlier than the Gospels.
    • Jesus is almost entirely non‑biographical.
    • Emphasis on visions and revelations rather than eyewitness testimony.
    • Theology is radically different from Jewish messianic expectations.
    • Christ resembles figures from Hellenistic mystery religions.

    This creates the impression that Paul was constructing a new religious figure.

  5. A more historically grounded interpretation

    A strong, evidence‑based synthesis is:

    There was a historical Yeshua, but Paul transformed him into a universal, divine Christ figure, creating the foundation of Christianity as we know it.

    This avoids the extremes of:

    • “Jesus was invented”
    • “Paul simply passed on what Jesus taught”

    Neither of those fits the evidence well.

  6. Where colonialism enters the conversation

    This is important:

    • Paul’s reinterpretation of Jesus was not colonial.
    • But later empires (Rome after Constantine, then European colonial powers) used Paul’s universalist, obedience‑focused theology to support imperial structures.

    So the colonial use of Christianity is real—but it happens centuries after Paul, not at the origin.

Paul's and Jesus' Core Teachings

  1. Jesus’s Core Teachings (as reconstructed by historians)

    Historians reconstruct Jesus’s teachings by looking for themes that fit:

    • 1st‑century Jewish context
    • Multiple independent sources
    • Teachings that would not benefit later Christian theology (criterion of embarrassment)

      • From this, Jesus appears as:

      • A Jewish apocalyptic teacher

        • He preached that God’s kingdom was coming soon to overturn injustice.

      • A reformer within Judaism

        He taught

        • Love of neighbor.
        • Radical forgiveness.
        • Nonviolence.
        • Care for the poor.
        • Purity of heart.
        • A return to the ethical core of Torah.

      • A critic of political and religious elites

        He challenged:

        • Roman imperial power.
        • Temple corruption.
        • Wealthy landowners.
        • Hypocrisy among religious leanders.

      • A teacher of deeds over beliefs

        Jesus emphasized actions, not doctrines:

        • Feed the hungry.
        • Forgive enemies.
        • Care for the maginalized.
        • Live righteously.
        • Trust God.

      • A message centered on the transformation of society

        His “Kingdom of God” was not about going to heaven—it was about justice on earth.

  2. Paul’s Core Teachings (from his letters)

    Paul’s writings are the earliest Christian documents, but they present a very different emphasis.

    Paul teaches:

    • A cosmic Christ

      • Jesus is a divine, pre‑existent being who died and rose to save humanity.

    • Salvation through faith in Christ

      • Paul's central message:

      Humans are saved by faith in Jesus’s death and resurrection, not by following the Torah.

    • A universal religion

      Paul removes ethnic boundaries:

      • Gentiles don’t need to follow Jewish law.
      • Circumcision, kosher laws, and Sabbath are unnecessary.
      • Faith replaces Jewish identity markers.

    • A focus on sin and redemption

      • Paul frames Jesus’s death as a sacrificial atonement for sin.

    • Obedience to governing authorities

      • In Romans 13, Paul tells believers to obey the Roman Empire—something Jesus never taught.

    Law (Torah) Upholds it (“not one jot or tittle will pass”) Declares it unnecessary for Gentiles
    Kingdom of God Imminent transformation of society Spiritual reality entered through faith
    Focus Deeds, compassion, justice Belief, grace, inner transformation
    Audience Jews in Israel Gentiles across the empire
    Politics Challenges empire and elites Advises obedience to empire
    Death of Jesus Result of political conflict Divine plan for atonement
  4. So what’s the big difference?

    The simplest way to put it:

    • Jesus preached a Jewish, ethical, anti‑imperial movement.
    • Paul preached a universal, theological, salvation‑by‑faith religion.

    They overlap in some areas, but their emphases are dramatically different.

  5. Why the difference?

    Because:

    • Jesus was a Jewish teacher living under Roman occupation.
    • Paul was a diaspora Jew shaped by Hellenistic culture and Roman citizenship.
    • Paul never met Jesus in life—his knowledge came from visions and interpretations.
    • Paul was trying to build a religion for the wider Greco‑Roman world.

    So Paul’s message naturally evolved in a different direction.

  6. Which version became Christianity?

    Paul;s

    Most of what modern Christianity teaches—atonement, salvation by faith, the divinity of Christ, the end of the law—comes from Paul, not Jesus.

    The Gospels were written later and often blend Jesus’s teachings with Paul’s theology.

Supporting Soulaan people

I'm hoping that after reading some, if not all, of the information on this page before this section it has been instilled in you an urge to support the melanin-rich ethnicity. I feel so angry about supporting a system that is determined to destroy us that I decided to try to do everything that I could to no longer support it.

I had to think about what to name this section. Black is a social construct built specifically to support White supremacy. I grow more and more sick of racial categories with every passing day.

I thought about calling it supporting people of African descent. But the truth is we are all of African descent because all of life began in Africa.

I thought about melanin-rich people, one that I have chosen to use in other places. It needs to be something like melanin-rich because all of us have melanin inside of us, including blue-eyed, blond-haired people.

It also needed to include the term ethnicity because that is exactly what all of us are. To make it specific to so-called Black people, I needed to combined melanin-rich and ethnicity.

Then I came across a website that I had forgotten about. It describes Soulaan people. Briefly, the term Soulaan was created because African-Americans have a unique identity within North America (U.S. soil) through our heritage of being formerly enslaved people, the effects of long-term oppression within the U.S., and the effects that long-term oppression has had on our cultural development, and social standing with ourselves and others. The creators of the term Soulaan believe we need a unique identifier that is not political or racially based. That identifier will do for this section. You can read more about the Soulaan identifier on the creators' website found in the Information & Support section of the list in this section.

The more I learn about the empire of White supremacy, all it's lies, distortions, exclusions, and fierce need to oppress us from all angles, It feels more and more important and urgent to support Soulaan people with every passing day. I have compiled this list to help me to accomplish that.
